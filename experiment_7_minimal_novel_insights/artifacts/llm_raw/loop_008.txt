- `childhoodbalancedpublic_original.csv` has ~14.4k respondents and 718 columns; of particular interest are the childhood media exposures (e.g., “during ages *0-12*: you spent time on the internet or computers (4tuoqly)”, the analogous 13–18 question, the “parents monitored/restricted your media intake to wholesome stuff… (vdounnv/3jmhhnr)”, the retrospective “Your parents gave useful guidance” item, and adult well-being measures such as “I love myself (2l8994l)”, “I am satisfied with my romantic relationships (hp9qz6f)”, “I am not happy (ix5iyv3)-neg”, and “I tend to suffer from depression (wz901dj)”. With Likert-style scoring from roughly –3 through +3, these give us both independent and dependent variables for a resilience-style model.

- The literature confirms the pieces we need to connect: Odgers & Jensen’s Annual Research Review (“Adolescent mental health in the digital age”, *JCPP* 61(3):336–348, DOI 10.1111/jcpp.13190) shows that digital technology use has at best small and mixed links with adolescent mental health, so long-term effects are understudied; Padilla-Walker & McLean’s JAH poster (Volume 64(S2):S110, DOI 10.1016/j.jadohealth.2018.10.233) documents that parental media monitoring improves adolescent mental health via reduced use; and Bellis et al.’s retrospective study in *BMC Psychiatry* 17:110 (DOI 10.1186/s12888-017-1260-z) argues that continuous trusted adult support buffers adults against adversity. Together these suggest we can extend the puzzle to adult self-love/happiness and romantic satisfaction.

- That gives us a novel angle: investigate how the combination of childhood internet/computer exposure and parental “wholesome” media restrictions—alongside retrospective judgments of parental guidance—predicts adult self-regard, romantic satisfaction, and depression-like experiences. In other words, does the same digital environment have different long-term consequences depending on whether parents tightly filtered media and provided consistent guidance? This interaction-level question, applied to adult outcomes, would extend the existing literature on adolescent digital media and parental mediation.
