**Literature Findings**
- `childhoodbalancedpublic_original.csv` contains 14,443 respondents and ~718 columns spanning demographics (age, gender identity, ethnicity, education), childhood context (family class, emotional/verbal abuse, guidance, purity/abstinence training for ages 0–12 and 13–18), adult outcomes (psychological distress, relationship and work satisfaction, sexual history, monogamy preference), and current religiosity—offering a broad cross-sectional view that can test how those childhood exposures map onto adult well-being.
- Recent peer-reviewed work shows purity-culture messaging contributes to shame, sexual dysfunction, and strained identity development, even beyond explicitly religious groups: the Journal of Sexual Medicine’s 2025 panel on “Being Pure and Being Ashamed” links purity culture intensity to lower sexual wellbeing (Coates et al., 2025), and Counseling Psychologist 2025’s “Being Yourself is a Sin” traces how evangelical purity teachings harm sexual- and gender-minority people socialized as women (Muskrat et al., 2025).
- Mental-health literature also implicates shame and internalized stigma in reduced adult well-being (e.g., International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health 2024 on coping with minority stress among queer young adults), suggesting purity messaging might operate through shame even for cisgender, heterosexual people, yet most existing studies focus on narrowly defined samples or single outcomes.
- This dataset lets us extend that literature by estimating, within a demographically heterogeneous survey, how childhood purity/abstinence indoctrination (0‑12 vs. 13‑18) relates to adult relationship satisfaction, self-worth, and mental-health items while controlling for other childhood family dynamics and current religiosity—thereby testing whether these associations hold for a broader population and whether later vs. earlier exposure matters.
