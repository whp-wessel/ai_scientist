- **Dataset & scope** `childhoodbalancedpublic_original.csv` contains ~14,444 respondents with both retrospective childhood exposures (e.g., “during ages *0-12*/13-18*: taught a purity culture that encouraged abstinence/waiting until marriage” along with abuse, parental guidance, religiosity, and socioeconomic context) and adult outcomes such as current anxiety/depression self-reports, relationship satisfaction, self-worth, and perceived treatment by social circles. This structure lets us compare developmental windows for purity-culture messaging while adjusting for co-occurring risk factors.

- **Literature grounding** Sexual shame/internalized stigma reliably predict internalizing symptoms and are often rooted in religious socialization (Tan & Anderson 2024, *Int. J. Environ. Res. Public Health*; Pachankis et al. 2023, *Clinical Psychological Science*). Early-life religious antecedents shape later spiritual well-being and mental health trajectories (Upenieks 2023, *Journal of Religion and Health*). Recent work also links purity-culture tropes to poorer marital/sexual satisfaction and pain experiences (Sawatsky et al. 2024, *Sociology of Religion*), supporting a focus on how those teachings ripple into adult relational evaluations.

- **Potential hypotheses** 1) Pre-adolescent (0–12) purity-culture instruction will be a stronger predictor of adult internalizing symptoms (depression/anxiety scales) than adolescent-only instruction, even after controlling for religious identity and abuse, because shame introduced in early identity formation is particularly durable (in line with Tan & Anderson, Pachankis, Upenieks). 2) Greater purity-culture exposure maps onto lower adult romantic satisfaction and weaker perceptions of being treated well, extending Sawatsky et al.’s findings beyond religion-specific samples to a broader, balanced cohort and testing whether the same messaging undermines social trust/self-worth.
