**Literature Summary**  
- Survey review: `childhoodbalancedpublic_original.csv` is ~14.4k respondents with ~700 columns covering demographics, current and childhood religion, mental health, consent experiences, relationship satisfaction, and paired childhood/adolescent context items (e.g., “taught a purity culture… (0-12)” vs. “(13-18)”). The romantic-satisfaction item `I am satisfied with my romantic relationships (hp9qz6f)` is on a −3/+3 scale; the adolescent purity-culture item averages −0.43 (sd 2.42) and has a modest bivariate correlation (r ≈ −0.02) with romantic satisfaction, leaving room to explore conditional associations once we control for confounders like trauma, age, gender, and current religiosity.  
- Background literature: abstinence-only/purity rhetoric has been linked to poorer sexual-health outcomes (Stanger-Hall & Hall 2011, *PLoS ONE* 6(10):e24658, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0024658), and religious belief alters how people interpret sexual functioning and satisfaction (Lazar et al. 2023/2024, *Mental Health, Religion & Culture* 26(8):786–801, DOI 10.1080/13674676.2023.2276105). These peer-reviewed findings justify focusing on purity-culture messaging and its current-religious context.

**Preliminary Hypotheses (novel angle)**  
1. **Timing effect:** Higher exposure to purity-culture messaging during adolescence (ages 13–18) predicts lower adult romantic relationship satisfaction even after adjusting for earlier childhood exposure, trauma, and demographics, because abstinence-only teachings appear to leave lingering sexual/relational consequences once individuals reach adulthood (Stanger-Hall & Hall 2011).  
2. **Religious-disengagement moderation:** The negative adolescent-purity effect is amplified for people who no longer practice religion (versus those still actively religious), since the dissonance between past teachings and current identity may intensify shame and reduce relational satisfaction; Lazar et al. (2023/2024) suggests that the meaning of sexual experiences depends on ongoing religious belief, so we can test whether current religiosity moderates the purity-culture signal.

These hypotheses leverage the survey’s unique dual timing (0–12 vs. 13–18) and current-religion measures to extend the existing literature rather than replicate it.
